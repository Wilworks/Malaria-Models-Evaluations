\begin{abstract}
Deep learning models for automated malaria diagnosis routinely report diagnostic accuracies above 95\%, but this evidence is drawn almost entirely from datasets collected outside Africa—most prominently the United States National Institutes of Health (NIH) malaria cell dataset collected in Bangladesh. No publicly documented study has measured how these models perform, without retraining, on Ghanaian-collected blood smear images. At the same time, existing literature asserts that such models suffer significant performance degradation under geographic domain shift, a claim resting largely on citation chains rather than direct empirical measurements against African data. This paper presents a zero-shot cross-domain evaluation of externally-trained malaria detection models against the Ghanaian subset of the Lacuna Malaria Dataset (Princess Marie Louise Hospital, Accra). Our objective is to produce the first direct empirical account of this generalization gap and translate the resulting failure patterns into actionable clinical deployment-safety guidance.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{M}{alaria} remains a major public health challenge in sub-Saharan Africa. Microscopic examination of Giemsa-stained blood smears remains the World Health Organization gold-standard diagnostic method. However, its diagnostic accuracy depends heavily on trained microscopists, a resource scarce in rural and primary care settings.

Computer vision models have been proposed to automate microscopic diagnosis, reporting high accuracies (95--99\%). However, almost none of this performance evidence has been validated on clinical data collected in African healthcare facilities.

\section{Gap in Existing Evidence}
The claim that malaria detection models degrade under domain shift is widely cited \cite{kabore2025addressing}. However, close inspection reveals that existing reviews rely on narrative synthesis rather than direct empirical measurement on African clinical samples. Furthermore, an audit of open repositories demonstrates an evidentiary asymmetry: most published peer-reviewed models release code without trained weights, whereas available pre-trained models are frequently informal or un-peer-reviewed artifacts.

\section{Research Questions}
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{RQ1}: What is the measured drop in sensitivity, specificity, precision, F1-score, and mAP when externally-trained models are applied zero-shot to Ghanaian clinical blood smear data?
    \item \textbf{RQ2}: What specific failure modes (species/stage confusion, parasite vs. leukocyte confusion, smartphone capture artifacts) characterize this performance drop?
    \item \textbf{RQ3}: What deployment-safety thresholds and evidence standards must precede clinical deployment in Ghana?
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset and Candidate Models}
This benchmark utilizes the Ghanaian subset of the Lacuna Malaria Dataset (3,000 thick smear images and 1,000 thin smear images, collected at Princess Marie Louise Hospital, Accra) \cite{makerere2024lacuna}. Candidates evaluated zero-shot include the peer-reviewed NIH MalariaScreener \cite{yu2020malaria} and open YOLOv8 object detection checkpoints.

\section{Methodology and Evaluation Pipeline}
All candidate models are evaluated strictly zero-shot without fine-tuning. Evaluation metrics include class-level sensitivity, specificity, mAP@0.5, mAP@[0.5:0.95], and quality-stratified error matrices.
